There are two things I need to understand. The first is habituation: is it how every cell
stores information? My guess is that a single cell can hold long-term memory, and that
habituation is the form it takes.

The second is what happens when that scales up, to neurons and to specialised cells like
photoreceptors. My picture is that every cell in the body stores memory, and the body shares it
like a decentralised network, pulling up what it needs when it needs it. The work of attending
to a stimulus, recalling, or expressing genes leaves a byproduct, and I think that byproduct is
long-term memory across the senses.

I also suspect this is coordinated by a morphogenetic rule: some mathematics tied to how many
cells there are and what it takes to hold them together. That rule would pick out the cells at
the edge, and give every cell a sense of where it sits in the whole. If so, memory and spatial
awareness aren't extras. They come built in once cells are coordinated.

Alone, a cell probably meets the world much as it does in a crowd. But pack in thousands or
millions, and network effects appear. Every cell polarises, trades ions and carries a small
electric field, so it can't help affecting its neighbours. Packed densely enough, it has to
pass on what it holds, and the network keeps passing it on until it looks like replay.

Honeybees are the comparison I keep coming back to. A colony gets big enough that it has to
communicate, and at some threshold it suddenly takes a shape. The shape I imagine is two
feedback loops running across the whole network, one for salience and one for habituated
memory. The shared signal might be what you get when each cell has to swap its habituated state
for a fresher one nearby: trading its version of the story for the waggle dancer's.

The last piece is the edge. I think the outer cells are the first line of defence. They sort
external reward and stimulus from things that can be habituated, or silenced. Silencing may be
their most important job: once the outside is ruled out, the whole system can spot what is
unusual inside itself. Vibration, bioelectric signalling, coupling and firing all have to be
damped, or the system feeds back on itself and loses coherence.

## Where I'd start

The pieces to simulate, or falsify: single cells save information; they coordinate it; each
knows its place in the network; and past some threshold of size, shape and complexity, a
network has to start communicating like this. The places to look are networks that did or
didn't cross that line: slime mould, honeybees, ants, termites, robot swarms.
